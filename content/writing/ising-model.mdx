The grid at the top of my homepage is a live Markov chain. It comes from a lecture in CS 4850, Mathematical Foundations for the Information Age, that stuck with me long after the semester ended.

## The idea

Each dot is a vertex labeled +1 (bright) or −1 (faint), and neighbors like to agree. A labeling x gets weight w(x) = exp(c · Σ x(u)x(v)) over the edges, where c is the inverse temperature. We want to sample from π = w / Z, but Z sums over 2ⁿ states. You can't compute it.

The trick is to never compute it. Pick a random vertex, propose flipping it, and accept with probability min\{1, w(y) / w(x)\}. Z cancels in the ratio, and the flip only depends on that vertex's four neighbors. Run the chain long enough and its state is a sample from π.

Near c ≈ 0.44 something sudden happens: noise snaps into large aligned domains. Hover over the grid to melt them, or click to cool a spot.

## On CS 4850

4850 has been my favorite class at Cornell. I didn't take it to satisfy a requirement. I took it because the description sounded cool. It was challenging to keep up with, but learning such unique topics was incredibly rewarding.

Rather than going deep on one subject, the course let me explore the combination of fields I find most interesting: linear algebra, probability theory, and algorithm design. This lecture is the cleanest example of all three meeting. A three-line algorithm, a proof that fits on a page, and out comes a phase transition.

It also changed how I think about my own work. When an LLM samples a token at temperature T, it's drawing from the same exp(−E / T) shape as this grid. Sampling from a distribution you can't normalize isn't just a classroom problem. It's at the heart of a lot of modern ML.
